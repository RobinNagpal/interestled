# ADHD Learning Guidelines

Why ADHD learners disengage, what the research says, and the general ways to fix it.
Companion to [README.md](./README.md).

ADHD is not a shortage of attention. It is attention that follows **interest,
novelty, urgency and challenge** rather than importance. Four traits shape every
point below:

- **Weak working memory.** Meta-analyses find moderate-to-large deficits, largest in
  spatial working memory (Martinussen 2005; Kasper 2012). Holding anything in the
  head is expensive.
- **Time blindness.** Time estimation and reproduction are reliably impaired
  (Toplak 2006). An unknown length feels infinite.
- **Delay aversion.** ADHD shows steeper delay discounting — a smaller reward now
  beats a bigger one later (Sonuga-Barke 1992; Marx 2021). A payoff next week does
  not motivate today.
- **Inconsistent performance.** The most robust lab finding in ADHD is not being
  slower but being *more variable* — reaction times swing far more, trial to trial
  and day to day (Kofler 2013).

Most of these fixes help everyone. They are only *essential* for ADHD learners.

**Each point has three parts:** what actually happens, the evidence, and general
ways to solve it. The solutions are deliberately medium-independent — they apply to
a book, a class, a video, a tutor or an app. What our own product does about them is
in one short section [at the end](#what-this-means-for-our-product).

### How to read the evidence tags

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[strong]** | Meta-analysis or a large replicated literature |
| **[moderate]** | Real studies, but fewer, smaller, or with mixed replication |
| **[inference]** | No direct study. Derived from a well-supported mechanism — a design bet, not a fact |

Full references at the end. Where a finding is contested, I say so.

## 20 things that work

### 1. Open with the most interesting thing, not the first thing

**What happens.** Attention is not handed out by a judgement about importance. It is
recruited by a felt gap — the sense that there is something here you cannot yet
explain. Telling someone a topic matters is an abstract argument competing with
everything else in their head, and it usually loses. Showing them something they
cannot account for creates an itch instead. In ADHD the importance route is weaker,
so the interest route carries almost the whole load.

**Evidence [strong].** Loewenstein's information-gap theory ties curiosity to a
perceived gap in one's own knowledge. Kang (2009) and Gruber (2014) found high
curiosity improves memory both for the answer and for unrelated material encountered
at the same moment, with matching activity in dopamine-linked midbrain and
hippocampus.

**How to solve it**
- Invert the usual order: lead with the puzzle, the anomaly, or the finished result,
  and let background arrive only when it is needed to resolve it.
- Create a need to know before answering it, so the explanation is received as an
  answer rather than as a lecture.
- Choose the entry point by interest, not by logical dependency. Logical order is
  right for reference material and wrong for learning.

### 2. Make the unit small enough to finish

**What happens.** Attention decays on continuous input, but the thing that matters
most is the *boundary*. Without endings, material becomes an undifferentiated stream
with no point at which anything can be banked as done. Before mastery arrives,
completion is the only reward available — remove it and there is nothing to work
for. Boundaries also create natural points to check whether anything went in.

**Evidence [strong].** Mayer's segmenting principle: the same material in
learner-paced chunks beats one continuous run. Guo (2014), across 6.9 million edX
video sessions, found median engagement peaks around six minutes and falls sharply
after — regardless of the video's total length.

**How to solve it**
- Segment by idea rather than by clock: one complete thought per unit, with an
  explicit end.
- Give each unit a stated outcome, so being finished is verifiable instead of
  arbitrary.
- Where material is unavoidably long, impose artificial boundaries — a question, a
  checkpoint, a summary — rather than leaving it continuous.

### 3. Say how long it takes before they start

**What happens.** Starting is a cost-benefit judgement, and when the cost side is
unknown the mind fills it in as "large". Someone who cannot estimate duration
internally has no way to bound that cost, so an unlabelled task reads as endless and
gets deferred. The same blindness applies during the task: with no sense of elapsed
time there is no sense of approaching the end, and no lift from being near it.

**Evidence [strong].** Time estimation and reproduction are impaired in ADHD
(Toplak 2006). Combined with delay aversion (Sonuga-Barke 2003), an open-ended task
is an unbounded cost against a distant reward — the worst possible shape.

**How to solve it**
- State the duration before asking for commitment, and keep the estimate honest. One
  inflated number destroys trust in every future one.
- Make time visible while working — a position marker, a page count, a bar — so time
  is read off the environment rather than estimated internally.
- Prefer bounded units to open sessions. "Four pages" is far easier to begin than
  "read for an hour".

### 4. Make the first action tiny and physical

**What happens.** The hard part is the transition into the task, not the task.
Starting means generating an action out of nothing; continuing only means not
stopping. A first step that contains a decision — "work out what to do first" —
reintroduces exactly the executive cost you were trying to avoid. A physical first
step also recruits motor initiation, which is easier to trigger than intention.

**Evidence [strong].** Gollwitzer & Sheeran's meta-analysis of ~94 studies found
implementation intentions — a specific "when X, I do Y" — produced a
medium-to-large effect (d ≈ 0.65) over intention alone, by removing the decision at
the moment of action.

**How to solve it**
- Shrink the first step until it is trivially doable: open the file, write one line,
  say one sentence.
- Decide the step in advance, so nothing has to be decided at the moment of doing.
- Set a floor rather than a target. Permission to stop after five minutes removes the
  dread that blocks starting, and most sessions run longer anyway.

### 5. Change format every few minutes

**What happens.** Repeating the same input shape produces habituation — the signal
stops driving a response even though it is still arriving. Novelty is a genuine
driver of dopaminergic activity, so varying the form of the material is a supply of
the thing attention runs on, not decoration. Under-stimulation is also actively
unpleasant: a bored learner will go and find stimulation somewhere else.

**Evidence [moderate-strong].** Zentall's optimal stimulation theory holds that ADHD
behaviour is partly a search for stimulation under low-stimulation conditions, and
that added novelty improves on-task performance. Bunzeck & Düzel (2006) showed novel
stimuli activate the substantia nigra/VTA — the same dopaminergic region implicated
in ADHD reward signalling (Volkow 2009).

**How to solve it**
- Vary the modality — read, watch, hear, do, say — not just the topic.
- Vary the *demand* as well: recognise, produce, judge, apply. A new demand refreshes
  attention even on the same content.
- Treat monotony as a fault in the material. If a stretch feels samey to the author,
  it is already much worse for the learner.

### 6. Give feedback quickly

**What happens.** Feedback works by attaching to the memory of the action that
produced it. As the gap grows, that link weakens and the learner has to rebuild what
they did before the correction means anything. Where reward sensitivity is
time-dependent, delay flattens the reinforcement almost completely.

**Evidence [moderate].** Luman's review finds ADHD performance is unusually sensitive
to reinforcement timing, and that delay degrades it more than in controls. Honest
caveat: in neurotypical lab studies, *delayed* feedback sometimes beats immediate for
complex material (Kulik & Kulik 1988), so this holds for practice and reps rather
than for essay-length work.

**How to solve it**
- Close the loop while the attempt is still live — same sitting at worst, same minute
  ideally.
- Prefer tasks where correctness is self-evident. Something that visibly works or
  fails needs no external marker at all.
- For long pieces of work, insert intermediate checkpoints instead of a single
  verdict at the end.

### 7. Let them move around the subject freely

**What happens.** A curriculum's order comes from the subject's logical structure.
Attention is allocated by the learner's current interest. When those diverge,
enforcing the order means teaching someone who is not attending, which teaches
nothing. The prerequisite worry is real but usually overstated — most missing
background can be supplied in a sentence at the moment it is needed, and partial
understanding is often enough to carry on with.

**Evidence [moderate].** Patall's meta-analysis (2008) found that providing choice
raises intrinsic motivation and effort. For ADHD, interest is what recruits attention
in the first place, so an order imposed against it costs more than the missing
prerequisite does.

**How to solve it**
- Make the structure visible and enterable from any point, with prerequisites shown
  as advice rather than as barriers.
- Supply missing background just in time and in the smallest useful amount.
- Treat where someone wanders as evidence of their real goal, and reorganise around
  it rather than correcting it.

### 8. Keep hands and mouth busy

**What happens.** Passive intake produces nothing to check understanding against, so
it is easy to sustain the feeling of following while retaining none of it. Producing
something forces retrieval, which strengthens memory far more than re-reading does.
For ADHD there is a second effect: movement appears to raise arousal to the level
where cognitive work is easier, which means restlessness during hard tasks may be
compensating rather than interfering.

**Evidence [moderate-strong].** Sarver & Rapport (2015) found that in children with
ADHD, *more* gross motor activity predicted *better* working memory performance. The
generation effect (Slamecka & Graf 1978) shows self-produced answers are remembered
much better than read ones.

**How to solve it**
- Convert consumption into production: explain it aloud, teach it to someone, work an
  example, predict the next step.
- Design for movement rather than against it — pacing, walking, standing, fidgeting.
  Stillness is not a precondition for focus and demanding it can cost performance.
- Use speech as an output channel. Talking is lower-friction than writing for many
  people and forces the same retrieval.

### 9. Keep everything needed in view

**What happens.** Working memory is a small, volatile buffer. Anything the learner
has to hold — a rule, a value, a goal — takes capacity that is then unavailable for
understanding. When two pieces of information must be combined but are presented
apart, effort goes into shuttling and re-holding instead of thinking. This is a load
problem, not a motivation problem, and no amount of trying harder fixes it.

**Evidence [strong].** ADHD working memory deficits are well established (Martinussen
2005; Kasper 2012). The split-attention effect (Chandler & Sweller 1992) shows that
forcing learners to integrate separated sources reduces learning independently of
content. Barkley's model treats externalising information as the primary
compensation for executive-function deficits.

**How to solve it**
- Externalise everything: write it down, keep it visible, and repeat rather than
  cross-reference.
- Put things that must be used together in the same visual field, integrated rather
  than merely adjacent.
- Give the learner a persistent artefact — a sheet, a board, a card — that carries
  the current state, so nothing important lives only in their head.

### 10. Add mild urgency

**What happens.** Performance depends on arousal, and a slow, low-pressure task
leaves arousal below the level needed for engagement. A modest deadline or a quicker
tempo pulls it up. This is the mechanism behind the familiar pattern of working well
the night before a deadline: the urgency is doing real neurochemical work, not
merely concentrating the mind.

**Evidence [strong].** Sergeant's cognitive-energetic model predicts performance
depends on event rate, and studies consistently find slow event rates hurt ADHD
groups disproportionately while faster rates narrow the gap with controls.

**How to solve it**
- Use short, bounded time challenges on practice and recall — never on first
  understanding (see anti-pattern 19).
- Raise the event rate: more items, shorter, in quicker succession beats fewer and
  longer.
- Create real stakes rather than artificial ones. Someone waiting, a commitment made
  out loud, or a booked session with another person all work better than a countdown.

### 11. Protect hyperfocus

**What happens.** Deep absorption is unusually valuable here and unusually fragile.
Entering it is unreliable, being knocked out of it is easy, and re-entering the same
day may be impossible. Standard productivity advice — fixed breaks, timed work
cycles — assumes focus can be summoned on demand, and that assumption is precisely
what does not hold. Breaking a rare productive run to follow a schedule trades a
large gain for a small one.

**Evidence [moderate].** Hyperfocus is now documented and measured in ADHD
(Hupfeld 2019; Ashinoff & Abu-Akel 2021) as intense, sustained absorption in a
high-interest task. The literature is young, but the practical implication is
consistent.

**How to solve it**
- Do not interrupt a productive run for a scheduled break. Break when the run ends.
- Remove interruptions in advance rather than resisting them in the moment; during
  absorption you will not notice them until they have already cost you.
- Keep the next piece of work ready, so the gap between pieces does not become an
  exit point.
- Set external boundaries for the things hyperfocus genuinely endangers — meals,
  sleep, hard appointments — but not on the work itself.

### 12. Make stopping safe

**What happens.** Resuming an interrupted task means rebuilding the goal state: what
you were doing, why, and where you had got to. That reconstruction is executive work,
and when it is expensive the task is quietly abandoned rather than resumed. The
anticipation of that cost also stops people beginning anything they cannot finish in
one sitting, which rules out most worthwhile things.

**Evidence [strong].** Altmann & Trafton's memory-for-goals model and Mark's
interruption studies (2008) both show resumption carries a real reconstruction cost.
For a working-memory-limited learner, that reconstruction is the expensive part.

**How to solve it**
- Stop somewhere that makes re-entry cheap — mid-example or mid-sentence, not at a
  clean but contextless boundary.
- Leave an explicit next action in writing. "Next I was going to…" is worth far more
  on return than a summary of what is already done.
- Preserve partial work by default, so stopping never carries a penalty and therefore
  never has to be avoided.

### 13. Show one thing at a time

**What happens.** Everything present competes for selection, and filtering is the
weak function. Extra material is not neutral — even when relevant and even when
interesting, it consumes selection effort and can crowd out the main line. The
finding that surprises people is that *interesting* extras hurt most, precisely
because they win the competition.

**Evidence [strong].** Mayer's coherence principle: removing
interesting-but-unnecessary material improves learning, across many replications.
The seductive-details effect (Rey 2012 meta-analysis) confirms attractive extras
actively cost comprehension.

**How to solve it**
- Remove rather than de-emphasise. If it is not needed for this idea, cut it; making
  it smaller does not make it free.
- Present one idea per view, with an explicit transition between ideas.
- Control the environment as deliberately as the material — sound, interruptions,
  visual clutter, competing screens.

### 14. Ask before explaining

**What happens.** An attempt made before the explanation changes what the explanation
does. It creates a specific gap, activates related knowledge, and produces a
prediction the explanation either confirms or corrects. Failing the attempt is fine
and may be the point — the error marks the spot the correction should land on. Given
the answer first, there is no gap and the material lands on nothing.

**Evidence [strong].** Pretesting works: Richland (2009) and Kornell (2009) found that
attempting an answer and failing before being taught produces better retention than
studying for the same time.

**How to solve it**
- Ask for a guess, a prediction, or an attempt before teaching — an uninformed one is
  still useful.
- Never penalise the guess. As soon as it is scored, people stop guessing honestly and
  the mechanism dies.
- Make the explanation address the gap directly: name what they expected and why the
  truth differs.

### 15. Use surprise and story

**What happens.** Memory is not uniform. Events that violate expectation or carry
emotional charge are encoded more strongly; neutral facts carry no such tag. A fact
that contradicts an assumption you just made is doubly encoded — as a surprise and as
a correction. Stories add a second advantage: causal links make each element
retrievable from the others, so remembering one part recovers the rest.

**Evidence [strong].** Dopamine neurons fire to *prediction error* — the gap between
expectation and outcome — rather than to reward itself (Schultz 1997). Emotional
arousal at encoding improves later recall through noradrenergic mechanisms (Cahill &
McGaugh 1995).

**How to solve it**
- Build explanations around contradictions of the obvious answer rather than around
  definitions.
- Attach material to consequences and people: what it cost, who it affected, what
  happened next.
- Use narrative order — situation, problem, attempt, outcome — rather than taxonomic
  order for anything that has to be remembered rather than looked up.

### 16. Reduce the number of decisions

**What happens.** Every choice point is a decision, and decisions are executive work.
When initiation is already expensive, a menu is where the session stops — the learner
defers rather than chooses badly. There is a genuine tension here, because choice
also raises motivation, and removing all of it creates a different problem. The
resolution is that choice should be *available but never required*.

**Evidence [contested — read the caveat].** Iyengar & Lepper (2000) famously found
shoppers bought jam ten times more often from a display of 6 than of 24. But
Scheibehenne's meta-analysis (2010) found the average choice-overload effect across
studies is close to zero and highly conditional. What survives is narrower and
sufficient: more options mean more decision time and more deferral, and deferral is
the failure mode that matters here.

**How to solve it**
- Always offer a strong default, so choosing is optional rather than the price of
  proceeding.
- Keep simultaneous options few, and make one visibly recommended.
- Separate deciding from doing in time: plan when energy is high, execute later
  without re-deciding anything.

### 17. Err on the side of too fast

**What happens.** Boredom and confusion have very different recovery profiles. A
confused learner slows down, re-reads or asks. A disengaged learner leaves and does
not come back. That asymmetry means the safer error is slightly too fast. Because
under-stimulation is actively unpleasant here rather than merely unproductive, a
comfortable pace for the average learner is often already past the point where this
one has gone.

**Evidence [moderate].** Sustained attention deficits in ADHD are well established
(Huang-Pollock 2012), and performance degrades with time on task. Zentall's work
indicates under-stimulation drives off-task behaviour.

**How to solve it**
- Pitch slightly above comfort and make depth available on demand, so nobody is held
  at the slowest pace in the room.
- Cut recaps, transitions and warm-ups — they lose a bored learner first.
- When someone drops out, check whether it was too slow before assuming it was too
  hard. The two look identical from outside and get opposite fixes.

### 18. Let them skip what they already know

**What happens.** Practising a mastered skill produces near-zero learning at a real
cost in engagement. Most courses over-practise heavily because they are built for an
average learner who does not exist. For an ADHD learner the damage goes further than
wasted time: being made to sit through known material teaches that this source is not
worth attending closely, and that lesson generalises to the parts that do matter.

**Evidence [moderate].** Cen (2007), using Cognitive Tutor data, showed students
substantially over-practise mastered skills and that removing the over-practice saves
large amounts of time with no loss of learning.

**How to solve it**
- Diagnose before instructing, and teach only the gap.
- Give an explicit, low-friction way to declare something known — and honour it
  rather than treating it as a claim to be disproved.
- Verify occasionally by spot-check rather than pre-emptively, so honesty is kept
  without forcing everyone through everything.

### 19. Make progress concrete

**What happens.** A percentage is an abstraction over a total the learner has no
feel for, and abstractions do not motivate. A statement of new capability is
concrete, checkable, and is the actual thing they came for. Progress information also
has a second job — effort rises as a visible endpoint approaches — and that only
works if the endpoint is credible.

**Evidence [strong].** Locke & Latham's goal-setting theory: specific, concrete goals
consistently outperform vague ones. Kivetz (2006) showed effort accelerates as a
visible endpoint nears.

**How to solve it**
- Express progress as things you can now do, not as material covered.
- Make the whole finite and visible from the start, so there is a real endpoint to
  approach.
- Show what remains as well as what is done. The remaining count is the half that
  drives the acceleration.

### 20. Make coming back after a gap painless

**What happens.** A lapse against a standard triggers a response out of proportion to
the lapse: the goal is judged already failed, so continued effort feels pointless and
the whole thing gets dropped. Where emotional regulation is harder, that moment
arrives faster and hits harder. The practical result is that any system which
punishes a missed day tends to lose people permanently over a single miss.

**Evidence [moderate].** The what-the-hell effect (Cochran & Tesser 1996) and the
abstinence-violation effect from relapse research describe the same pattern. Emotion
dysregulation is present in a large share of adults with ADHD (Shaw 2014), which
raises the cost of that moment considerably.

**How to solve it**
- Design for the return rather than for the perfect run. Assume gaps and make
  re-entry the well-trodden path.
- Never let a lapse destroy accumulated progress. Keep consistency and achievement as
  separate things, so losing one does not erase the other.
- On return, offer a short reload of context rather than a backlog of what was missed.
  A pile of overdue work is a wall, and people do not climb it.

## 20 things that do not work

### 1. Long unbroken text

**What happens.** Reading a long block means continuously re-selecting the text over
intrusive thoughts. Mind-wandering rises with time on task while comprehension falls,
and the characteristic failure is that the eyes keep moving with nothing going in —
often unnoticed until pages later. Elevated spontaneous mind-wandering makes that
window much shorter.

**Evidence [strong].** Mind-wandering increases with time on a text and predicts worse
comprehension (Smallwood & Schooler 2006). Bozhilova's review (2018) finds
spontaneous mind-wandering is elevated in ADHD and tracks symptom severity.

**How to solve it**
- Break text with structural landmarks — headings, lists, tables, diagrams — so there
  are re-entry points after a drift.
- Insert questions through long text so intake gets checked before the drift compounds.
- Convert dense prose into a picture or a worked example where possible; both resist
  drift better than paragraphs.

### 2. Payoff only at the end

**What happens.** A distant reward is heavily discounted and does not compete with a
small immediate alternative. So "this will all make sense at the end" is not
motivating however true it is. The stretch of unrewarded effort before the payoff is
exactly where people stop, and the fact that they were nearly there makes no
difference.

**Evidence [strong].** ADHD shows steeper delay discounting across many studies
(Marx 2021 meta-analysis).

**How to solve it**
- Distribute the payoff so every segment produces something usable on its own.
- Shorten the loop between effort and visible result, even artificially.
- Prefer many small completions to one large one, even at some cost in elegance.

### 3. Tasks with no defined finish

**What happens.** An ambiguous task makes the learner define the task themselves,
which is planning work stacked on top of learning work. "Review this chapter" has no
completion test, so there is no way to know when you are done and no way to judge
whether starting is worth it. Vague goals also measurably underperform specific ones
regardless of anyone's attention profile.

**Evidence [strong].** Goal-setting research consistently finds vague goals ("do your
best") produce worse performance than specific ones (Locke & Latham).

**How to solve it**
- Convert every intention into a task with a completion test: say what will exist when
  it is done.
- Replace open verbs — explore, review, get familiar with — with a countable output.
- Where the task is genuinely open-ended, impose an arbitrary boundary: three
  examples, one page, twenty minutes.

### 4. Locked steps and enforced sequence

**What happens.** A gate turns a moment of live interest into a chore. Someone who
wants to know how one thing works is handed four unrelated units first, and the
interest — which was the entire resource — is spent before the answer arrives.
Enforcement also treats prerequisites as binary, when in practice a rough grasp is
usually enough to proceed and gets sharpened by use.

**Evidence [moderate].** Choice supports intrinsic motivation (Patall 2008), and
enforced sequence works directly against the interest that is doing the attentional
work.

**How to solve it**
- Advise rather than enforce: name what is missing and let the learner decide.
- Allow understanding to be provisional — partial now, refined on return.
- Provide several entry points into the same material at different depths, so the
  answer to "I'm not ready" is a shorter version rather than a redirection.

### 5. Warm-ups, preambles and objectives

**What happens.** Attention is highest at the start and declines from there. Spending
that peak on history, aims and housekeeping means the real content arrives when
capacity has already dropped. A preamble also delays the moment the learner can tell
whether this is even the thing they wanted, which is when most of them leave.

**Evidence [inference, from a strong base].** No study measures preambles directly.
But sustained attention declines with time on task (Huang-Pollock 2012) and curiosity
has to be established to get its memory benefit (Gruber 2014), so the opening minutes
are both the most valuable and the most easily wasted.

**How to solve it**
- Front-load the substance and deliver context only when it becomes necessary.
- Drop objectives and agendas; if an outcome needs stating, one sentence covers it.
- Test the opening by asking whether a knowledgeable person would skip it. If they
  would, everyone will.

### 6. Long video

**What happens.** Video imposes the author's pace. You cannot skim it, scanning back
is clumsy, and there is no natural checkpoint to notice you have stopped taking
anything in. It also produces an unusually strong illusion of learning — watching
feels like intake while yielding little, so the learner is doubly misled about how
well it went.

**Evidence [strong].** Guo (2014) found engagement drops sharply past ~6 minutes and
shorter videos are watched far more completely. Szpunar (2013, PNAS) found inserting
short tests into a lecture segment roughly halved self-reported mind-wandering and
improved final test performance.

**How to solve it**
- Keep video short and single-purpose, and use it where motion is genuinely necessary
  — a process, a demonstration, a physical technique.
- Always pair it with text, so pace and search return to the learner.
- Interrupt longer recordings with questions. This is a tested fix, not a guess.

### 7. Note-taking as the main activity

**What happens.** Transcribing and understanding compete for the same resources.
Notes taken while trying to follow tend to be verbatim and shallow — the effort feels
productive but is closer to copying than to processing. And in most cases the notes
are never opened again, so the whole cost buys nothing.

**Evidence [moderate].** Piolat (2005) shows note-taking imposes high cognitive load
that competes with comprehension. Honest caveat: the well-known longhand-beats-laptop
result (Mueller & Oppenheimer 2014) failed to replicate at scale (Morehead 2019), so
do not over-claim the handwriting part.

**How to solve it**
- Separate capture from understanding: get the record from somewhere else and spend
  the learner's effort on retrieval instead.
- If notes are taken, take them *after* a segment from memory. That turns note-taking
  into a retrieval exercise rather than a transcription one.
- During input, mark and question; write properly afterwards.

### 8. Relying on the learner to come back

**What happens.** Remembering to do something at a future moment is a specific
weakness, distinct from motivation. The intention is genuine; it simply fails to
surface at the moment it is needed, and the person is often surprised and embarrassed
when they realise. Any system that depends on this fails silently, because nobody
reports the sessions that never occurred to them.

**Evidence [strong].** Prospective memory is reliably impaired in ADHD (Talbot &
Kerns 2014, and meta-analytic reviews since).

**How to solve it**
- Externalise the reminder into the environment: alarms, calendar entries, visible
  physical cues, or another person expecting you.
- Attach the new activity to an existing reliable routine rather than to a clock time.
- Make the cue specific and actionable. A vague nudge does not survive contact with a
  busy moment; "do the two-minute review" does.

### 9. Streaks and daily guilt

**What happens.** Streaks work through loss aversion, which requires the streak to be
losable — so the mechanic is strongest immediately before it destroys itself. It also
moves motivation from the material to the counter, and when the counter goes the
reason to continue goes with it. For someone whose consistency is intrinsically
variable, the mechanic is calibrated to fail.

**Evidence [moderate].** Deci's meta-analysis of 128 studies found tangible,
performance-contingent rewards reliably undermine intrinsic motivation. Combined with
the what-the-hell effect, a streak first replaces interest with obligation and then
punishes a single miss. Honest note: I know of no direct trial of streak mechanics in
ADHD populations — this is two strong findings pointed at one feature.

**How to solve it**
- Reward the act, not the chain: count sessions cumulatively so a gap subtracts
  nothing.
- Keep motivation attached to the subject itself, since external motivators can crowd
  out interest rather than adding to it.
- Build recovery into any consistency scheme. A miss should have a defined,
  non-punitive next step.

### 10. Dead time

**What happens.** An unfilled gap is an invitation to switch away, and switching away
is far easier than switching back. The cost is not the seconds — it is the session,
because the return trip has to pay the whole resumption cost from point 12. Waiting
also breaks the chain of thought the next piece depended on.

**Evidence [moderate].** Nielsen's response-time limits are the standard: about one
second keeps thought uninterrupted; about ten seconds is the limit of attention on a
task. Industry latency and abandonment data points the same way.

**How to solve it**
- Remove waiting from the path wherever possible; prepare materials before the session
  rather than during it.
- Where waiting is unavoidable, fill it with something related rather than with
  nothing.
- Keep attention engaged through transitions instead of leaving a blank at exactly the
  moment focus is most fragile.

### 11. Several instructions at once

**What happens.** A multi-step instruction must be held in working memory while step
one is carried out. Capacity is exceeded and everything after the first clause is
lost. From outside this looks like not listening or not caring; it is a buffer
overflow, and repeating the same instruction louder does not add capacity.

**Evidence [strong].** Following multi-step spoken instructions is a well-studied
working-memory task, and performance tracks working-memory span closely (Gathercole
2008) — precisely the capacity reduced in ADHD.

**How to solve it**
- One step, then wait for it to be done, then the next.
- Write multi-step processes down so they are read rather than held.
- Have the learner restate or tick off steps, so the list lives outside their head
  where it can be checked.

### 12. Rules that must be carried across pages

**What happens.** When a rule appears on page two and is needed on page five, the
learner either flips back — losing the thread — or reconstructs it from memory,
losing accuracy. Either way effort goes into logistics rather than the idea, and the
resulting difficulty is easily mistaken for the material being hard.

**Evidence [strong].** The split-attention effect (Chandler & Sweller 1992): information
that must be used together but is presented apart imposes load and measurably reduces
learning.

**How to solve it**
- Integrate: put information where it is used, even at the cost of repeating it.
- Duplicate essentials freely. Redundancy is cheap; lookups are expensive.
- Where reference is unavoidable, make it permanently visible rather than something to
  navigate to.

### 13. Cluttered material and environments

**What happens.** Filtering out irrelevant stimuli is the weak function, so a busy
field imposes a continuous tax that never shows up as a discrete problem. Attractive
irrelevant material is the worst kind, because it wins the competition against the
main line — which is why "making it more engaging" by adding things often backfires.

**Evidence [strong].** The coherence and seductive-details literature (Mayer;
Rey 2012 meta-analysis) shows added interesting-but-irrelevant material lowers
comprehension.

**How to solve it**
- Remove rather than de-emphasise. Small clutter is still clutter.
- Treat the physical environment as part of the material and set it up deliberately.
- Reduce ambient interruption in advance — notifications, passers-by, background
  activity — rather than trying to resist it in the moment.

### 14. Setup before starting

**What happens.** The motivation to begin arrives in bursts and does not keep. Any
required preliminary — configuration, tidying, planning, gathering materials — spends
that burst, and the actual work never starts. Worse, preliminaries feel productive, so
they make good substitutes for the work and can absorb an entire session without
anyone noticing.

**Evidence [inference, from a strong base].** Task initiation is the documented
bottleneck in ADHD executive function, and each added pre-step is another initiation
cost paid before any reward arrives.

**How to solve it**
- Make it possible to start with zero preparation; keep a setup that is permanently
  ready to go.
- Do preparation at a different time from the work, so it never blocks a moment of
  willingness.
- Watch for productive-feeling preliminaries and name them for what they are.

### 15. Repeating what they already know

**What happens.** No learning, real cost. Beyond the wasted time, boredom here is an
exit rather than an inefficiency, and it trains the learner to attend loosely to this
source — which then damages the material that actually needed attention.

**Evidence [moderate].** Over-practice of mastered skills is measurable and wasteful
(Cen 2007). Under-stimulation drives off-task behaviour (Zentall).

**How to solve it**
- Assess before teaching, every time.
- Treat skipping as a right rather than a concession.
- Vary the challenge instead of the repetition: apply a known idea in an unfamiliar
  context rather than restating it.

### 16. Vague progress numbers

**What happens.** A percentage of a total the learner cannot see is noise. It does not
tell them whether to continue, and it cannot produce the end-of-goal acceleration
because there is no credible end in view. Worse, progress numbers that move without
anything real happening actively destroy trust in every other signal.

**Evidence [strong].** Goal specificity drives performance (Locke & Latham), and the
goal-gradient effect needs a legible endpoint to work against (Kivetz 2006).

**How to solve it**
- Make the whole finite and visible from the outset.
- Measure in units the learner recognises as real — chapters, skills, problems solved.
- Prefer a checklist of concrete items to a bar, and never let the indicator move
  without a real change behind it.

### 17. Harsh marking

**What happens.** Feedback aimed at the person rather than the work redirects
attention from the task to self-evaluation, which reliably damages performance. Where
emotional responses are stronger and slower to settle, that can end the session
outright and colour the decision about whether to come back at all. The information in
the feedback is usually fine; the framing is what does the damage.

**Evidence [strong].** Kluger & DeNisi's meta-analysis of over 600 effect sizes found
roughly a third of feedback interventions *reduced* performance, with self-directed
feedback the main culprit. Emotion dysregulation affects a large share of adults with
ADHD (Shaw 2014).

**How to solve it**
- Keep feedback about the work, specific, and paired with a next action.
- Say what is right before what is missing. Both are information, but the order
  determines whether the second half gets heard.
- Separate practice from assessment. Practice that is scored stops being practice.

### 18. Assuming today will match yesterday

**What happens.** Variability is intrinsic here, not a sign of fading commitment. A
system tuned to yesterday's capacity will be wrong on most days — too hard on the bad
ones, too easy on the good ones. Reading a bad day as backsliding is both inaccurate
and demoralising, and it prompts exactly the wrong response: more pressure on a day
when capacity is already low.

**Evidence [strong].** Elevated intra-individual variability is among the most
replicated findings in ADHD (Kofler 2013 meta-analysis).

**How to solve it**
- Set a low floor and an open ceiling: a minimum achievable on a bad day, with room to
  run on a good one.
- Adapt to the current session rather than to a rolling average.
- Judge by cumulative output over weeks rather than by day-to-day consistency, which
  measures the wrong thing.

### 19. Timing the thinking

**What happens.** Time pressure consumes working memory with monitoring and worry —
the exact resource comprehension needs. This causes choking specifically on tasks that
depend on that resource, and it hits hardest the people relying on deliberate
strategies. Note this is not a contradiction of "add urgency": urgency motivates
*starting*, while a clock on *thinking* blocks it. The distinction is what is being
timed.

**Evidence [strong].** Beilock & Carr (2005) showed pressure causes choking on
working-memory-dependent tasks, hitting high-working-memory strategies hardest.

**How to solve it**
- Time only what speed is genuinely part of — recall, fluency, a practised procedure.
- Never time first exposure or reasoning-heavy work.
- If pacing is needed, bound the whole session rather than the individual thought.

### 20. "Just focus" and effort framing

**What happens.** Telling someone to focus asks them to solve, through effort, a
problem in the mechanism that regulates effort. It also implies the difficulty is a
character flaw, which adds shame to a task already being avoided and makes the next
attempt less likely. And it contains no information: nothing about what to do
differently.

**Evidence [moderate-strong].** ADHD has measurable neurobiological correlates,
including reduced dopamine reward-pathway markers in adults (Volkow 2009, JAMA).
Effort-blaming feedback is also self-directed feedback — the category Kluger & DeNisi
identified as most likely to hurt performance.

**How to solve it**
- Change the conditions rather than issuing exhortations: smaller step, different
  format, different time of day, different environment.
- Treat a stall as information about the design of the task, not about the person.
- Where difficulty is persistent and pervasive, especially alongside low mood, that is
  outside the scope of study technique. The right move is professional support, not a
  better plan.

## What this means for our product

Six concrete edits to the main design:

- **Nodes get shorter** — 3 minutes, not 8 — and each shows its minute cost up front.
- **The map never locks.** Missing prerequisites are a note on the node, not a gate.
- **Format rotates within a session**, enforced by a same-format counter.
- **Review is opt-in and tiny** — three items, not twenty — and skipping costs nothing.
- **Return is a restore point**, not a summary: what you were doing, one tap to resume.
- **No streaks anywhere.** Progress is stated as new ability, never as days in a row.

Two existing features already do this job and should be protected: **depth buttons**,
which let a sudden interest run as far as it wants, and **explain-back**, which is
active production and holds attention far better than any reading screen.
